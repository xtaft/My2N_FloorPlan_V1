# Floor Plan Visual Layer — Architecture

How a PDF becomes an interactive, access-aware floor plan, and why the data is
shaped the way it is.

---

## The one rule behind everything

**Store only what cannot be derived. Derive everything else.**

Three facts are stored:

- a **wall** holds the two rooms it separates — it *is* the shared boundary
- a **door** holds the wall it sits in
- a **room** holds the group it belongs to

Everything else — room shapes, which doors a room has, which groups a door
connects, which rooms are neighbours, outer walls, entrances — is computed on
demand.

This is not about saving space. A building is a few thousand small records and
every derivation is a scan over tens of items. It is about **editing**.
Merging flats, splitting rooms, moving a room to another group, adding and
removing walls — each one touches many relationships at once. A stored
relationship can be left stale by a missed update, and a stale access rule is
invisible: the map looks right and quietly lies about who can get in.
A derived relationship cannot go stale.

---

# Part 1 — Segmentation

Each floor is uploaded as its own PDF and processed on its own. The floors are
stitched together afterwards.

## Per floor

### 1. Find the rooms

The segmentation service returns one polygon per room. It does not know what
any of them are — just outlines. Inner walls are included, so a flat arrives as
several rooms, not one.

### 2. Snap the corners

Two rooms that share a wall should share the *same* corner points, but
segmentation gives each room its own, slightly off. Nearby points are snapped
together and stored once, in a global vertex table.

Without this, two rooms drift apart under editing and leave gaps invisible at
normal zoom.

**Vertex hygiene** — four rules that hold for the whole lifetime of the plan, not
just at import:

- **Never create a vertex outside the lookup.** A quantized key map,
  `f"{floor_id}:{round(x/EPS)}:{round(y/EPS)}"` with `EPS ≈ 1` canvas unit, is
  the only way vertices come into existence. `split_wall`, add-wall, and import
  all go through it.
- **Namespace the key by floor.** Two floors both containing `(0,0)` would
  otherwise collide and fuse geometry across storeys — producing a wall shared
  between two different floors' rooms, which nothing downstream would catch.
  Floors generated by cloning a template must remap vertex ids, not reuse them.
- **Never move a vertex.** Vertices are shared, so moving one reshapes
  neighbouring rooms. This is the reason wall dragging is out of scope.
- **Garbage-collect degree-0 vertices** after any merge or dissolve.

### 3. Cut the shared edges

One room's long wall may face two rooms on the other side:

```
Room A:  (0,0) → (0,2)
Room B:  (0,0) → (0,1)
Room C:  (0,1) → (0,2)

becomes  wall (0,0)–(0,1)  between A and B
         wall (0,1)–(0,2)  between A and C
```

Overlapping edges are split so that every wall has exactly one room on each
side. This is the step that makes "which shared segment is this" a question
with an answer.

### 4. Build the wall graph

Each wall now knows its two rooms; each room knows its set of walls. Room
polygons are **no longer stored** — they are worked out from the walls when
needed.

Wall direction is fixed by convention here: walking `v1 → v2`, the left side is
`room_a`, the right is `room_b`. Exterior is always `room_b`. Every later
operation inherits this, so "which side" never needs asking again.

### 5. Read the door symbols

Doors are drawn with a swing arc. The arc gives four things at once:

- **position** along the wall (`t`)
- **width** — the leaf length
- **hinge end** — where the arc is centred
- **swing side** — which room it opens into

That fills the whole door record from the drawing. Without symbol detection all
four would default and need correcting by hand, which is a lot of clicks across
a building.

Openings with no arc — archways, the gap between a lobby and its lift —
appear as plain gaps. These become doors with `kind = "opening"`: a wall, a
position, a width, but no leaf, so `hinge` and `swing` are null.

Distinguish this from `wall.state = "opening"`, which is a *deleted* wall.
A door-kind opening is a doorway in a wall that still exists; it stays in the
adjacency index, is an access edge, and can carry a device. A wall-state opening
means the wall is gone. Conflating them would drop every archway out of the
access graph.

**Doors never split their wall.** A wall with a door is still one wall with two
vertices; the gap is computed when drawing. Remove the door and the wall is
whole again, with nothing to rejoin.

## Then the user

### 6. Mark the hallways

The manager points at the corridors. This is the one thing no algorithm can
guess — corridors look like rooms, and there is no drawing convention for
"circulation". Everything downstream depends on it.

### 7. Flood fill into apartments

From each hallway door, walk through interior doors until another hallway or
stairwell door is reached. Everything collected becomes one group.

This is a **proposal**, not a result:

- two flats joined by a connecting door come out as one group
- a flat with a missed interior door comes out as several
- a flat with two doors to the same hallway is reached twice (dedupe)

The manager confirms or corrects. Grouping is just a field on each room, so
correcting is cheap.

### 8. Tag the shafts

Lifts (rectangle with crossed diagonals) and stairs (parallel treads with a
direction arrow) are usually marked in the drawing, and are suggested from
those symbols plus footprint. The manager confirms, and each gets a
**shaft identity** — a `shaft_id` shared by that shaft's group on every floor.

Confirmation is kept rather than skipped: a wrong auto-tag fabricates vertical
reachability, which is worse than one extra click. The stair arrow is a bonus —
it says whether a staircase serves the floor above, below, or both.

## Once all floors are in

### 9. Fit the floors together

PDFs differ in page margins and sometimes in drawing scale. Scale is measured
from the **distance between two shaft centroids**, this floor against the
reference:

```
scale = dist(shaft_a, shaft_b) on this floor
      / dist(shaft_a, shaft_b) on the reference floor
```

A two-shaft baseline is ~20m rather than a single lift core's ~2m, so the same
absolute segmentation error produces ten times less scale error. With one shaft
only, fall back to its dimensions and treat the result as a hint.

```
0.95 < scale < 1.05   →  snap to 1.0   (segmentation noise, not real)
scale outside 0.5–2.0 →  flag for review (probably matched the wrong shaft)
```

Then shift and rotate so the shafts line up. Stored as a per-floor
**Align** fits a floor onto the one below and, in the same gesture, records
which shaft is which — overlap at the moment of the drag is the pairing, so a
shaft that lands on nothing simply has no partner and its links stop there.
Two decisions the gesture leaves open, settled here:

- **The proposed scale is what to apply, not what was measured.** The ratio
  between the two shaft baselines is a measurement; a floor drawn 1.5x the
  reference has to come back to 0.667 to fit. The readout says "scaled to
  112%", which has to mean the floor is drawn at 112% — so the proposal is
  the reciprocal of the ratio, and a test asserts that applying it actually
  makes the two baselines agree.
- **The single-floor map does not apply the transform.** It shows one floor,
  whose transform the viewBox normalises away for everything except rotation,
  and every tool's hit-testing works in floor coordinates. A floor aligned at
  an angle therefore draws square on the map and correct in align mode. Worth
  fixing when a view shows two floors at once; not worth inverting every
  pointer conversion before then.

`transform (dx, dy, rotation, scale)` — never baked into vertices, so alignment
stays adjustable and errors do not accumulate. Uniform scale only; independent
x/y scaling would make walls non-perpendicular.

The manager sees the result over a ghost of the floor below and can nudge.
Most floors need no touch — the job becomes verification, not alignment.

### 10. Link the floors vertically

For each shaft, sort its groups by storey `level` and link consecutive pairs
with a **virtual door** — no wall, just a connection:

```python
members = sorted(groups_with(shaft_id), key=lambda g: floor(g).level)
for lower, upper in zip(members, members[1:]):
    link(lower, upper, kind="virtual")
```

Consecutive pairs only. A door from L1 straight to L4 would let the access
search skip floors, and would break the moment an intermediate level is
restricted.

Readers go on the **physical** door from hallway into the shaft, not on the
vertical links — that is where the hardware actually is, and it means one
device guards the whole shaft. It also means regenerating vertical doors never
destroys a device.

### 11. Check nothing is stranded

Run the access search from each floor's hallway with no restrictions. Anything
not reached is a segmentation error — usually a flat whose only door was missed.
Flag it now rather than letting a manager find it weeks later.

This check matters more than it looks, because access is evaluated **per room**.
A missed interior door is no longer harmless: previously a flat was a single
access unit, so everything inside it was reachable regardless of whether its
bedroom doors were detected. Now an undetected bedroom door means that bedroom is
genuinely unreachable in the model. Run the check at room level, not envelope
level.

# Part 2 — The objects

```python
@dataclass
class Vertex:
    id: str
    x: float
    y: float

@dataclass
class Wall:
    id: str
    v1: str                  # walking v1 → v2, room_a is on the left
    v2: str
    room_a: str              # a room id, or "exterior"
    room_b: str
    state: str = "solid"     # or "opening" — knocked through, record kept

@dataclass
class Room:
    id: str                  # the access node — BFS runs over these
    name: str
    walls: set               # unordered — the shape is derived from these
    group_id: str            # which envelope it sits in
    space_type: str = None   # usually None — inherited from the envelope
    floor_id: str            # denormalised, for search without walking the tree
    surfaced: bool = False   # visibility only, no access meaning

@dataclass
class Group:
    id: str                  # the envelope: a flat, a gym, a hallway, a shaft
    name: str
    space_type: str          # "apartment", "circulation", "gym", ...
    parent_group_id: str = None   # envelopes nest; None → top level
    floor_id: str
    sort_index: float        # position in the navigation drawer
    domain_ref: str          # the platform's apartment/unit entity
    shaft_id: str = None     # set on shaft groups only

@dataclass
class Door:
    id: str
    name: str
    kind: str = "physical"   # "physical" | "opening" | "virtual"
    wall_id: str = None      # physical and opening
    t: float = None          # 0..1 along the wall — centre of the opening
    width: float = None      # canvas units, the same ones the vertices use
    hinge: str = None        # physical only — which end the arc centres on
    swing: str = None        # physical only — which room it opens into
    rooms: tuple = None      # virtual only — no wall, so the pair is stored

@dataclass
class Device:
    id: str
    name: str
    device_type: str         # "intercom", "reader", "keypad", ...
    host_kind: str           # "room" or "door"
    host_id: str = None      # None → orphaned, sits in the unplaced list
    position: tuple = None   # x,y — room-hosted only
    guards: tuple = ()       # door-hosted — room ids it checks entry into
    floor_id: str = None
    status: str = "ok"

@dataclass
class Floor:
    id: str
    name: str
    level: int               # the real storey — never changed by dragging
    sort_index: float        # drawer position only
    transform: tuple         # dx, dy, rotation, scale

@dataclass
class Profile:
    id: str                  # a permission SET, not a person
    devices: list            # the device ids this profile opens
    people: list             # who holds it — a count for the UI, nothing more
```

Lengths — `width`, and every coordinate — are in **canvas units**, the ones the
vertices are in. A floor's `transform` carries the scale that relates them to
the world, which is exactly why it is stored per floor and never baked in.
Nothing in the model converts to metres; a dimension readout would be the first
thing that has to.

A **serialized** plan may also carry fields that are reports rather than model
state — a `counts` block, an `unplaced_devices` list, the rooms a profile's
devices happen to guard. Every one of those is derivable, so by the rule above
none of them is model state: recompute them on load and never read them back.
They are written for the person reading the file, and they are the first things
to go stale when the file is edited by hand.

## What is deliberately absent

| Not stored | Derived from |
|---|---|
| room polygons | the room's walls, by angular traversal |
| a door's room pair | `wall.room_a`, `wall.room_b` |
| a room's doors | doors on the room's walls |
| a room's access type | the room's own, else its envelope's |
| an envelope's outline | its rooms' outer walls |
| an envelope's outer walls | walls whose two rooms are in different envelopes |
| an envelope's entrances | doors with exactly one side in the envelope |
| which rooms are neighbours | shared walls |
| door endpoints | `t`, `width`, and the wall's vertices |
| a device's side of a door | `guards` against the wall's room pair |

## The two lookups everything uses

```python
def rooms_of(door, walls):
    if door.kind == "virtual":
        return door.rooms              # no wall — the one stored case
    w = walls[door.wall_id]
    return (w.room_a, w.room_b)        # ask the wall

def access_type(room, groups):
    return room.space_type or groups[room.group_id].space_type
```

A room normally has no type of its own, so all four bedrooms in flat 3B inherit
`apartment` from their envelope. A locker room with its own reader gets
`space_type` set explicitly and overrides. Typing a room is the exception, not
the rule — without inheritance every room in the building would need one.

`access_type` answers *what kind of space is this*, which drives colour,
default visibility and how a result is reported. It does **not** decide who may
enter: that is the profile's device set, checked against the devices on the
door. The name is older than the rule and is kept only because it is what the
field is called.

## Which side is which

Every "side" question — which room, which way the leaf swings, where a reader is
drawn — resolves from the wall's direction. No side is stored anywhere except as
`a`/`b`.

**The convention.** Walking `v1 → v2`, the left side is `room_a`, the right is
`room_b`. Exterior is always `room_b`.

```python
def normal(wall, vertices):
    """Points toward room_a. SVG's y axis grows DOWNWARD, so left-of-travel
    walking v1 → v2 is (dy, -dx) — not the (-dy, dx) of a y-up system."""
    a, b = vertices[wall.v1], vertices[wall.v2]
    return (b.y - a.y, -(b.x - a.x))
```

The sign is worth stating explicitly: plans come from SVG, where y increases
downward, so every intuition carried over from mathematical coordinates is
inverted. Getting it wrong produces swing arcs and reader positions that are
consistently on the wrong side — visible, but easy to mistake for a rendering
bug rather than a convention error.

Flip a wall's direction and `room_a`/`room_b` must swap with it, so the two stay
locked together. Fixed once in segmentation step 4; `split_wall` and dissolve
both inherit it, since every fragment keeps the parent's `v1 → v2` sense and its
room pair.

Worth asserting in a debug build after every operation: cheap to verify, nasty to
debug once violated.

**Door orientation** is two characters, both from the drawing's swing arc:

| Field | Values | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `hinge` | `"v1"` / `"v2"` | which end of the wall the arc is centred on |
| `swing` | `"a"` / `"b"` | which room the leaf opens into |

```python
def door_geometry(door, wall, vertices):
    a, b   = vertices[wall.v1], vertices[wall.v2]
    L      = dist(a, b)
    half   = (door.width / 2) / L
    p1, p2 = lerp(a, b, door.t - half), lerp(a, b, door.t + half)

    hinge_point = p1 if door.hinge == "v1" else p2
    n           = unit(normal(wall, vertices))          # toward room_a
    open_side   = n if door.swing == "a" else negate(n)
    return hinge_point, open_side
```

So the swing arc renders without any geometry being stored, and the user's
"flip" control is a single character toggle. Four combinations cover every real
door: hinge left or right, opening into either room.

When reading the arc from the drawing in step 5, map it to the **room** first and
then to `a`/`b`. The arc's geometric side does not necessarily match the wall's
direction, because the wall's direction was set by the orientation convention,
not by the drawing.

**Device side follows from `guards`**, never stored:

```python
def device_side(device, door, wall):
    a, b = rooms_of(door, wall)
    # the side access is verified FROM: the one the reader does not guard
    return "b" if a in device.guards else "a"
```

A reader sits on the side access is **verified from**, which is the side it does
not guard. Guarding `room_a` means you stand in `room_b` and present your
credential to get in, so the reader hangs on the `room_b` face. A reader inside
the room it protects would protect nothing. A stored side would go stale the
moment a wall's room pair changed; `guards` holds room ids, which is what
traversal reads anyway.

**Slit walls** (`room_a == room_b`) have no meaningful side and carry no doors —
a cut through a door is rejected. Just guard the renderer against a zero normal.

## Shapes, when needed

A room's walls are an unordered set. To draw it, walk them:

1. Build vertex → incident walls, using only this room's walls. A wall with
   `room_a == room_b` (a slit) is inserted twice, once per direction.
2. Walk: arrive at a vertex, leave by the next wall **in angular order** —
   the one immediately clockwise from the reverse of the arrival direction.
3. Continue to the start; that is one ring. Repeat until all traversals used.
4. Signed area per ring; the largest `|area|` is the outer one.

Angular order is what makes slits, dangling stubs and pinch points come out
right. Arbitrary choice produces figure-eights.

**Counting rings without extracting them.** For validation before committing an
operation, the number of independent cycles is cheaper than a full walk:

```
rings = E - V + C          # E walls, V distinct vertices, C connected components
```

`rings == 1` is a simple room; `rings > 1` is a donut. O(E), no geometry. Useful
for checking what an edit is about to do — a merge that is about to lose a hole,
or a cut that is about to turn a ring into a C.

Rings render as one path with multiple subpaths and `fill-rule="evenodd"`, so
a courtyard or a lift core inside a car park is a hole without special-casing.
Point-in-polygon for hit-testing and device placement must use even-odd too, or
a device dropped in the courtyard is assigned to the hallway around it.

Memoise per room against a version counter, bumped whenever the room's walls or
their vertices change. The cost is not in computing a shape — it is in
computing it on every render frame.

Labels use pole of inaccessibility, not centroid: a ring-shaped room's centroid
lands in the hole, and an L-shaped one's lands outside.

# Part 3 — Why this structure works

## Adding a wall

The only geometric primitive is `split_wall`:

```python
def split_wall(wall, point, doors):
    vm  = get_or_create_vertex(point)         # deduped against existing
    cut = fraction_along(wall, point)

    w1 = Wall(v1=wall.v1, v2=vm,      room_a=wall.room_a, room_b=wall.room_b)
    w2 = Wall(v1=vm,      v2=wall.v2, room_a=wall.room_a, room_b=wall.room_b)

    for d in doors_on(wall):                  # doors follow their wall
        if d.t < cut:  d.wall_id, d.t = w1.id, d.t / cut
        else:          d.wall_id, d.t = w2.id, (d.t - cut) / (1 - cut)

    for r in {wall.room_a, wall.room_b}:
        rooms[r].walls -= {wall.id}
        rooms[r].walls |= {w1.id, w2.id}
```

Adding a wall is then: split at each endpoint, create the connecting wall, and
walk the boundary from one endpoint to the other.

- **reaches the second point** → the room divides in two; collected walls go to
  the new room, untraversed rings are assigned by even-odd containment
- **returns to the starting directed edge first** → no division. The room was a
  ring and now has a slit, or two holes merged. Both are valid outcomes

Termination is on the starting **directed edge**, not the starting vertex: a
pinch point appears twice in one traversal and a vertex test would false-positive.

Doors need no reassignment in either case. They are on walls, and walls carry
the room pair.

Because `t` is rescaled rather than a position being re-matched, a door stays
exactly where it was. Storing door endpoints instead would mean the door's
position and its wall could disagree.

## Importing a space list

A list of apartments and rooms arrives from a letting system or a
spreadsheet long before anyone draws it. One row is one space: **Name**
required, **Floor**, **Type** and **Note** optional. Matching ignores case,
surrounding space and diacritics, because a list typed by hand and plans
exported from CAD will not agree on any of the three.

A row is matched in three steps, and a space is claimed once so two rows
cannot both take it:

1. a space **on the plan**, within the floor when the row names one — it is
   renamed and its type is set
2. a space **already in the structure** waiting to be placed — the row
   updates it, and its devices stay
3. **nothing** — the space joins the list still to be placed

```python
unplaced_spaces: list   # id, name, floor_id, space_type, note
```

A space with no shape cannot be a Room. A Room **is** its wall set, and one
with no walls breaks every derivation that assumes an outline — so these sit
beside the rooms exactly as an orphaned device sits beside the placed ones,
and the tree lists them under their floor so the import is visible somewhere.

**Placing one is step 1 done by hand.** A space waiting on the list is
dragged from the types panel, where it sits under the category it was
imported as, onto a space on the plan — or armed from its menu and given
the next one clicked: that room takes its name, category and note,
and the space leaves the list. It refuses a room on another storey, since a
row that named a floor meant it.

Rooms are the only thing that can receive one. An envelope is a set of
spaces rather than one, and a door is not a space at all, so neither has a
shape to give.

Setting a room's category is the same act however it is reached — the menu,
an imported list, or placing a space onto it — so it carries the same
consequence everywhere: a room that no longer shares its envelope's category
leaves it. Without that, an import would be the one way to leave a technical
room sitting inside an apartment.

**Nothing is deleted, ever.** A name or a type is replaced only where a row
matched, which is what makes importing the same list twice an update rather
than a second copy of the building. A blank Type column says nothing, which
is not the same as saying unknown: the category is left as it was. A row
naming a floor or a category this building does not have comes in without
one and is counted in the summary shown before anything is written.

The type named in a row is matched against **this building's categories**,
not a fixed list. A template that offers Office and Retail unit is advice to
whoever fills it in; what a space can actually be is whatever the types
panel holds.

### Making a category

**New category** makes an empty one and puts the row straight into rename,
with the placeholder name selected so the first keystroke replaces it. The
name is the only thing the user came to the button to decide, and a row
called "New category" is not an answer to it.

Its colour is picked at random from the palette, skipping every colour the
building already uses, so two categories made in a row are not born the
same shade. Once the palette is exhausted it repeats rather than refusing:
a duplicate colour is a smaller problem than not being able to make a
category.

**A category is listed while it holds something** — including a space that
is only waiting to be placed, so unassigning the last Technical room does
not take Technical away while its space is still somebody's to deal with.
A category that holds nothing is a type used a level further in, if at
all: `locker_room` is an override on rooms inside the gym, and those rooms
belong under Gym rather than in a category of their own.

A category just made is the exception, and the drawer carries it rather
than the derivation. The file cannot tell an empty new category from an
unused old one — both are a type nothing refers to — but the drawer knows
which one appeared in front of the user a second ago, so it keeps that one
on screen until something is in it. Nothing is stored for this, which is
the trade: reload before filling it and the row is gone, though the
category is still there to be found by anything that looks it up.

## Before there is a floor plan

The plan is not where the product starts. A customer arrives with their
apartments, their residents, their operators and their devices already in
My2N, and no drawing of any of it. The floor plan is the thing they are
about to make, and the first screen is the building without one.

That screen holds nothing of its own. Every list on it is derived from the
same file the plan is drawn from, with the plan ignored, so the building
seen before drawing and the building edited afterwards cannot drift apart:

| List | Derived as |
|---|---|
| spaces | the envelopes, flat — no floors, because which storey each is on is what the plan is for |
| residents | the people whose access reaches an apartment, in that apartment |
| operators | everyone else, ranked into one property manager, two on maintenance, the rest employees |
| devices | answering units in their flat; everything else unplaced |

**Answering units are placed and nothing else is**, and that is not a
simplification. A unit belongs to a flat, so it is placed from the day the
flat exists. A reader or an intercom goes on a door, and until the building
has been drawn there are no doors — which is the honest state, and also the
clearest statement of what the floor plan is worth.

Two things the data had to settle rather than a rule. Residents and staff
are numbered separately in the fixture, R001… and S001…, so a name handed
out by the digits in an id collides the two lists; position in a sorted
list cannot. And of the common spaces, the lobby is one a customer
administers while the four hallways are not — the difference being that
every resident is admitted to the lobby and nobody is admitted to a
hallway, because walking down it needs no permission.

### Making the plan

Four steps, and the list of floors is the whole state of them. It is plain
data with plain functions over it; each modal and the check screen are
views of that list, and none of them owns it.

1. **How.** Upload PDFs, or draw manually. Asked only when the way in did
   not already say: the empty state's two buttons each name a method, so
   they skip this.
2. **What.** Pages, in order, highest on top. However many arrive, the
   list settles on the building's five storeys: fewer and there would be
   storeys with nothing to check, more and there would be pages with no
   floor behind them. Padding goes underneath and the ground floor moves
   with it, or one uploaded page would sit at ground level with four
   basements below it. In the product this is however many were uploaded. The PDF path wants vector
   plans to detect from; the manual path takes any image to draw over, adds
   blank floors, and will go on with nothing at all. One floor is the
   ground floor — exactly one, reasserted after every add and remove,
   because the storey numbers are counted from it and a floor below it is
   a basement.
3. **Wait.** Detection, one floor at a time, closable.
4. **Check.** Below.

### Checking a floor

Detection is a guess, and this is where somebody reads it. Three things, in
the order the panel lists them: which spaces are shared, which are stairs
and lifts, and whether the floor sits over the one below.

**No segmentation happens here.** The spaces are the ones the model already
holds; what the screen adds is a menu to correct their categories, and that
menu calls the same operations the editor's menus do. Marking is undoable
because it is an ordinary edit.

**Only the three marked kinds carry colour.** Everything else is drawn as
walls alone — still clickable, but bare. The panel says anything left
unmarked stays a room or an apartment, so painting those would argue with
the instruction, and a plan where everything is coloured makes the three
that matter impossible to pick out. The fills are the legend's own values,
read from one list, so the swatch beside the instruction and the space on
the plan cannot say different things.

A floor is checked by being looked at. Opening it marks it, and the plan
cannot be saved until all of them have been — asking for a second click to
confirm you looked would be asking twice, and letting it save with floors
unread would make the step decorative.

**A floor lines up with the one checked before it**, not with the storey
underneath. The previous floor is the one the user has just decided is
right, so it is the only thing worth lining up with; walk the list from
the bottom and the two coincide, jump about and the previous one is still
the right answer.

Its lift shafts and stairs are outlined on the plan the whole time, not
only inside the align tool — "this floor should line up with the one
below" is a thing to notice rather than a thing to go looking for. The
outlines are carried into this floor's own coordinates, out of the other
floor's transform and back through this one, so where they sit is exactly
how far out the fit is. Floor 1 of the fixture is stored a little off
centre, because a step that is always already correct demonstrates
nothing.

Moving and aligning are the same tool, because moving a floor over the one
below *is* aligning it. A floor detection found nothing on is not an error:
the page is kept, the panel says so, and the layout can be drawn over it in
the editor.

Saving hands over to the editor, which is everything the rest of this
document describes.

## Deleting a space — which removes a name, not an area

A space cannot simply go. The floor is tiled, so its area would belong to
nobody; giving an area away is what removing a wall does. What can go is
everything that says what the space *is*.

| Intent | Operation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| this is not a room we track | delete | out of the tree, off the map |
| the plan is right that something is here, wrong about what | unassign from map | splits in two |

**Delete** strips the name to `Unnamed N`, clears `space_type` and
`group_id`, and unsurfaces it. With nothing of its own and no envelope to
inherit from it reads as Unknown, which is the bucket the types panel keeps
open at zero for exactly this.

**Unassign separates what a space is from where it is**, because the two
claims fail independently: the drawing was right that there is a space
there and wrong about which one, so neither half should be thrown away.

Which half is the new record matters, and it is the identity:

- **the room stays**, the same record with the same id, the same walls and
  the same doors, stripped of everything that said what it was. It is
  `Unnamed N` of no category, which resolves to Unknown. It is in the tree,
  in the drawer under Unknown, and in the access check, because it is
  still a space — one nobody has identified. That is a state a manager has
  to resolve, and a row they cannot see is not a to-do.
- **the identity leaves** as a new unplaced space: no walls at all, the
  original name, the category it read as, waiting in the drawer. It is not
  in the tree, because a space with no shape is not part of the floor's
  structure.

Minting the new record for the identity rather than for the shape is what
makes the rest fall out. Walls, doors and the readers that guard them go on
naming the same room id, so nothing has to be rewritten to point somewhere
new and nothing can be missed — unassign touches `rooms`, `groups`,
`devices` and `unplaced_spaces`, and no geometry at all.

Two spaces in a category are still two after one is unassigned: one placed
and one not. Each row says so — `N spaces | M unplaced`, M of the N, the
second half dropped when it is zero — and the room left behind is counted
under Unknown instead, because that is now what it is. A category is still
listed while everything in it is unplaced, or unassigning the last
Technical room would take the Technical category away with it.

Devices divide by what they belong to. An answering unit is the flat's
rather than the walls', so it comes away with the identity and returns when
that is placed again; the space carries their ids. A reader is on a door,
the door is part of the room, and the room has not moved — so it stays
exactly where it is, now verifying entry into an unnamed space.

Placing the space back onto a room joins them: that room takes the name,
the category and the note, and the units come home. It need not be the room
it came from — an identity with no shape goes wherever a shape is free.
What placing does not restore is envelope membership: the room left its
flat when it stopped agreeing with it, and rejoining a name is not
rejoining a flat.

An envelope emptied by either goes with it. The device inside a space stays
exactly where it is — a space losing its name is not a reason to lose
hardware — and undo restores all of it, which is the other reason the
record survives rather than being removed.

**Deleting an envelope** is a different thing again: it dissolves, and its
rooms move up to whatever contained it. Deleting an envelope cannot mean
deleting the spaces drawn inside it.

### Doors are selected in structure mode, and nowhere else

A door is a piece of structure, so it is clickable where structure is
edited. In devices mode a click near a doorway is after the device on it,
and the two selections would fight over the same few pixels. Structure
mode also stops drawing the devices for the same reason: a reader's
marker sits exactly on top of the door being edited.

Its menu is Flip wall side, Flip opening side, Delete. Both flips exist
because a door's orientation is read off the drawing's swing arc during
segmentation, and an ambiguous arc produces a confident wrong answer.
Nothing else in the model can tell which way a leaf opens, so nothing
else can correct it.

**A flip is about the drawing, never about access.** It says which way the
leaf really opens; it does not say who may go through. So a reader on a
flipped door comes out verifying the same room and drawn on the same side
of the wall. That holds because the side a reader is drawn on is derived
from its `guards` and not from the door's `swing` — the two are
independent on purpose, and a test pins it, alongside one asserting the
drawing *does* change, so the pair cannot both pass by nothing happening.

### Devices mode

The dropdown swaps what the plan is about, and with it the right-hand
panel. Structure mode lists the categories and hides the devices; devices
mode lists the devices and draws them **with their status**, which is the
one place in the editor where a device's health is the subject rather
than noise.

The panel is a flat list, not a tree: a device is attached to one thing
and the row says which — the storey and the space, or "Unassigned".
Grouping them by space would bury the unassigned ones, and those are the
whole reason the panel exists.

A device's menu is Rename device, then Unassign from door or Unassign
from space depending on what it is on, greyed when it is on nothing. The
kind of host survives being unassigned, so the greyed item still names
the right thing rather than guessing. Unassigning keeps the record and
drops the guards with the place: a reader verifies entry into a room
*through a door*, and off the door there is no entry left for it to
verify, so keeping the room would claim a check nothing carries out.

### Placing a device by dragging it

An unplaced device is dragged out of the panel onto the plan. Only
unplaced ones can be picked up: moving one that is already somewhere
means taking it off first, which is a decision worth making on purpose.

What the plan offers depends on what is in the air, because the two
kinds of device are not placed in the same sort of place. A reader or an
intercom gets **the free sides of every doorway** — two light halves
where nothing reads yet, one where something already does, none where
both are taken. An answering unit gets the spaces, because a unit is not
something you badge at.

**Which half you drop on is the whole of the choice.** It is the side the
device reads from, and that fixes the room it checks as the one on the
other side: you present your card where you are standing to get in to
where you are not. The half is drawn centred on the opening and bulging
to its side, so it sits exactly where the marker will.

A room that was folded away inside its flat is surfaced by a reader
landing on its door. Once something checks the way in, that way in is a
thing the manager has to see — but it stays in its envelope, because
gaining a reader does not make a bedroom stop being part of the flat.

**A short arrow crosses the doorway** from the device to the room it
admits you to. Nothing else on the plan says which way the check runs:
the marker is symmetrical and the room beyond looks like the room
behind. The arrow is derived, not stored — a door device is drawn one
offset to its own side of the opening, so the vector from the opening to
the marker, reversed, is the way in. A device in a space gets none,
because it checks no way through.

**Devices get hit targets only in devices mode.** Markers take no pointer
events everywhere else, so that a reader sitting on a doorway cannot
swallow the click meant for the door. Devices mode has no door selection
to protect — and a marker on an exterior door sits outside every room, so
there is nothing underneath it to carry the click anyway.

## Removing a wall — which is how rooms merge

**There is no standalone merge function.** Two rooms become one by deleting the
wall between them. That is the only way, and it keeps the two ideas from
diverging: the user's mental model ("take this wall out") and the data operation
are the same thing.

Two different user intents, two different operations:

| Intent | Operation | Rooms |
|---|---|---|
| knock through, keep both spaces | `wall.state = "opening"` | stay separate |
| these are really one space | delete the wall | merge |

An **opening** renders as a gap, passes freely, and keeps the record — so
"restore wall" works and dividing along that line later still works. That is the
right choice for a doorway-sized gap or a wall segmentation hallucinated.

A **deletion** is the merge:

```python
def delete_wall(w, rooms, doors, devices, groups):
    a, b = rooms[w.room_a], rooms[w.room_b]
    if a is b:
        return remove_slit(w)                  # a cut wall, no merge involved

    shared = walls_between(a, b)
    if runs(shared) > 1 and not encloses(a, b) and not encloses(b, a):
        reject("These rooms touch in two places — merging would leave a gap "
               "between them.")

    for door in doors_on(w):                   # only the clicked wall goes
        for dev in devices_on(door):
            dev.host_id, dev.guards = None, ()         # orphaned, not deleted
        delete(door)
    a.walls.discard(w.id); b.walls.discard(w.id)
    delete(w)

    for wall in shared - {w}:                  # the rest stay, as slits
        for door in doors_on(wall):            # which have no side to open between
            for dev in devices_on(door):
                dev.host_id, dev.guards = None, ()
            delete(door)

    a.walls |= b.walls                         # survivor takes the rest
    for wall in b.walls:                       # remap the room pair
        if wall.room_a == b.id: wall.room_a = a.id
        if wall.room_b == b.id: wall.room_b = a.id

    move_devices(from_=b, to=a)                # room-hosted ones
    a.group_id = surviving_envelope(a, b, groups)      # see below
    delete(b)
    dissolve_collinear(endpoints_of(shared))
```

Five things that operation has to get right:

**Only the clicked wall goes.** Two rooms often share a wall split into
fragments at T-junctions, and taking the whole run on one click demolishes
masonry nobody pointed at. The fragments left behind have the merged room on
both sides, which is a slit — a shape the model already renders and traverses
— and on the plan it is simply the stub of wall still standing. Removing those
too is another click each.

**Doors on retained walls survive; doors with one room on both sides cannot.**
A door from either room into the corridor must still be there afterwards. The
one in the deleted wall goes with it, and so does any door left on a fragment
that has become a slit: a slit has no two sides for a door to open between.

**Devices on those doors orphan, never delete.** `host_id = None`, `guards = ()`,
and the device appears in the unplaced list. A reader is a provisioned physical
asset — deleting it because a wall was removed would lose a real device and make
undo impossible.

**One name survives, and the wall says which.** The merged room keeps a single
name, and which one is not inferable from the shapes — a kitchen merged with a
dining room could reasonably be either. The wall itself settles it: `room_b`,
the side on the right walking v1 → v2. Same for `space_type`. Asking is worse
than a rule here, because the question comes up on every merge and the answer
is almost never interesting; a caller that does care can still say.

**A category of its own takes a room out of its envelope.** An envelope says
what the spaces inside it are, so a room that no longer agrees is no longer one
of them: giving a bedroom the technical category detaches it from the flat, and
an envelope left empty goes. Inheriting is not disagreeing — a null category is
the room saying the envelope decides. The detached room then belongs to no
envelope, which is why the types panel lists a category's loose spaces beside
its envelopes; counting envelopes alone would show the change nowhere.

**Envelope membership resolves by type precedence.** Three cases:

| Rooms in | Result |
|---|---|
| one envelope, one unassigned | joins the envelope |
| an apartment and a plain group | **the apartment wins** |
| two of the same kind | the right-hand room's, as with the name |

```python
PRECEDENCE = {"apartment": 2}            # everything else: 1, unassigned: 0

def surviving_envelope(a, b, groups):
    ga, gb = groups.get(a.group_id), groups.get(b.group_id)
    pa, pb = rank(ga), rank(gb)
    if pa != pb:
        return (ga if pa > pb else gb).id          # decided
    return rooms[w.room_b].group_id                # the surviving room's
```

An apartment outranks a plain group because it is the stronger claim: it has a
`domain_ref`, residents, and billing attached, where a generic group may be only
visual organisation. A space that now opens into a flat is part of the flat.

Two apartments, or two plain groups, are decided the same way the name is: the
room on the right of the wall keeps its envelope. Merging two flats does change
what the apartments *are*, so `domain_ref` still wants re-binding afterwards.
Mark the losing envelope's `domain_ref` orphaned rather than hard-deleting it,
so undo stays possible.

An envelope left with no rooms after the merge is deleted, and its own
`domain_ref` orphaned the same way.

**Void rejection.** Two rooms that meet on either side of a third wrap around
it, and once they are one room their walls close a loop around that third. An
even-odd fill then paints it as part of the merged room while it carries on
being its own space. Reject, and name the room that would be swallowed rather
than reporting a generic failure.

`runs(shared) > 1` is the cheap signal for this, but it is only a proxy: it
asks how the rooms touch rather than what the merge produced. Build the merge
and test the outcome — does any neighbour's label point now fall inside the
merged room's rings — and the answer is exact, and names the room. Only a
neighbour can be caught this way, since whatever closes around it is made of
the merged room's own walls.

Where one room fully encloses the other — a lift core inside a car park — the
merge is valid and the result simply loses a hole. Ring count goes from 2 to 1,
and ring extraction reports that without being told.

After any operation that deletes walls or rewrites a room pair, run
**collinear dissolve** over the affected endpoints:

```
dissolve vertex v when:
  degree exactly 2 (globally, not just within one room)
  the two walls are collinear within epsilon
  identical room pair as an unordered set
  v is not a door's anchor
```

Without it wall counts only grow. Merge four flats and each outer face carries
a dozen collinear fragments — hover highlights in pieces, and "how many walls
does this room have" becomes meaningless.

A degree-2 collinear vertex whose walls have *different* room pairs is a real
junction and must stay. Dissolving it would silently reassign ownership.

## What happens to a device when its host goes

Devices are **orphaned, never silently deleted**. A reader is a physical object
provisioned in the platform; destroying its record because someone edited a wall
would lose a real asset and make undo impossible.

```python
@dataclass
class Device:
    host_kind: str            # "room" or "door"
    host_id: str = None       # None → orphaned
    guards: tuple = ()
```

**Door deleted** → every device hosted on it gets `host_id = None` and
`guards = ()`. The device survives in an *unplaced* list, and the manager either
re-places it on another door or removes it deliberately.

**Wall turned into an opening** (`state = "opening"`) → its doors are removed, so
their devices orphan the same way. The wall record survives, so restoring it is
possible, but the door and its placement are gone.

**Wall hard-deleted** → doors cascade, devices orphan.

**Wall split** → nothing orphans. Doors follow their fragment by `t`, and devices
follow their door.

**Collinear dissolve** → nothing orphans. The rule excludes a vertex that is a
door's anchor, and `t` is rescaled across the rejoined wall.

**Room deleted or merged away** → room-hosted devices move to the surviving room.
Door-hosted devices whose `guards` named the vanished room have that entry
dropped; if `guards` becomes empty the device still exists on the door, now
checking nothing, and should be flagged rather than deleted — an unguarded reader
is a configuration error worth surfacing, not a record to throw away.

**Group split or merged** → nothing happens to devices at all. Grouping is one
field on a room, and devices are hosted on rooms and doors, not groups.

The unplaced list is the important half of this. Without it an orphaned device is
invisible: still provisioned, still consuming a licence, impossible to find on
the map. With it, "3 devices not placed" is a state a manager can resolve.

## Adding spaces to categories

Type is a field; colour comes from the type:

```python
space_types: id → { id, name, color }
group.space_type = "apartment"
```

A room or group holds no colour of its own, so the types panel owns rename and
recolour, and every space of that type follows. Renaming one space changes its
`name` only.

**`space_type` does not drive access.** A profile is a set of **device ids**,
so no category string appears in traversal at all — the search asks whether the
profile holds the device guarding the door, and nothing else.

It has to work that way. Every flat in the building is `space_type =
"apartment"`, so a permission expressed as a set of types would open all of
them to anyone who can open one. Access is per unit, and the only thing that
distinguishes A1's entrance from A2's is which reader it is.

What `space_type` does drive is colour, default room visibility, and how a
result is reported. That is still worth inheriting — see `access_type` below —
it is simply not what the search consumes.

Deleting a type must force reassignment. An untyped space has undefined
rendering and reporting.

## Viewing what someone can reach

Two graphs, and keeping them apart is what makes this simple:

- **containment** — a tree: `floor ⊃ group (envelope) ⊃ room`. Rendering,
  selection, drawer. Envelopes have no access role at all
- **access** — a graph over **rooms**. Every room is a node; every door is an
  edge. Cycles are normal

Rooms stay inside their envelope permanently — a locker room that gets a reader
is still part of the gym in the tree and on the map. That is only possible if
access is checked at room level, because the envelope is no longer the unit of
control.

Build the index once per topology change:

```python
def build_adjacency(doors, walls):
    adj = {}
    for door in doors.values():
        a, b = rooms_of(door, walls)
        adj.setdefault(a, []).append((door, b))
        adj.setdefault(b, []).append((door, a))
    return adj
```

Nothing is dropped. The bedroom doors inside a flat are real edges — they just
carry no device, so they are traversed freely and the result matches what
envelope-level traversal would have given. The difference appears only where a
device sits on an interior door, which is exactly the case this model exists to
handle.

Then breadth-first search:

```python
def reachable(start_room, profile, adj):
    seen    = {start_room}
    via     = {start_room: None}        # which door got us here
    blocked = {}                        # where we were turned away
    queue   = deque([start_room])

    while queue:
        here = queue.popleft()
        for door, there in adj[here]:
            if there in seen:
                continue                            # loops handled

            guards = [d for d in devices_on(door) if there in d.guards]
            if guards and not any(d.id in profile.devices for d in guards):
                blocked[there] = door
                continue                            # and everything past it

            seen.add(there)
            via[there] = door
            queue.append(there)

    return seen, via, blocked
```

Five things carry the weight:

- **`there in seen`** — buildings have loops. A square block around a courtyard,
  or a ring hallway cut in two places. Not a tree
- **no checks → free passage** — the stated model: a door with no reader is open
- **`there in d.guards`** — only devices guarding the side being *entered*.
  Walking the other way, they do not apply. Free egress, as in a real building
- **`d.id in profile.devices`** — the test is which reader, not which kind of
  space. Access is per unit: every flat is `space_type = "apartment"`, so a
  check against the type would open all of them at once
- **`blocked`** — remembers which door refused. This is what answers
  "why can't they get in", which is the question managers actually ask

`exterior` is a real sentinel room with no polygon, so the front door is an
ordinary door with a reader and entry needs no special case. Seed from whichever
side is not exterior.

Vertical travel is not special either. Virtual doors sit in the same index, so
lobby → flat 3B is five uniform edges:

```
lobby → lift L1 (physical, reader) → lift L2 (virtual) → lift L3 (virtual)
      → hallway L3 (physical) → flat 3B (physical, reader)
```

**Highlighting** is a lookup — `seen.has(room.id)` — because the traversal
output is directly the render input. Three states are worth distinguishing:
reachable, **blocked at a named door** (actionable), and never examined because
something upstream was blocked (not actionable). Rendering the last two the same
grey leaves a manager with ten dim shapes and no idea which one to fix.

Because the result is per room, report it at **envelope level by default** —
*Gym: partially accessible* — and expand to rooms on click. A resident blocked at
a locker-room door inside a gym they can otherwise enter is a different situation
from being blocked at the gym entrance, and both now appear in the same result.
Showing every room's state at once makes the map noisy in exactly the buildings
where this matters.

Walking `via` backwards gives the route, which explains an answer rather than
just asserting it.

Cost is O(rooms + doors) — thousands rather than hundreds now that rooms are the
nodes, which is still nothing for BFS. Group residents by their permission set
rather than iterating people: a building has a handful of distinct profiles, not
hundreds. Cache on `(profile, graph_version)`.

## Showing and hiding rooms

Rooms default to hidden; the group is what the map shows and what the user
operates on. A wall is drawn when:

```
room_a and room_b are in different groups
  OR either room is surfaced
```

One derived rule, no flags on walls.

**Surfacing follows device placement.** A locker room in a gym is visible
because its door has a reader; the fitness rooms beside it never are, because
nothing guards them. The manager never thinks about surfacing — they place the
hardware, and the map reflects it.

Surfacing is **purely visual**. A surfaced room does not leave its envelope and
does not change the access graph, because the access graph already contains every
room. Placing a reader therefore restructures nothing — it adds a device and
reveals the rooms that device sits between.

Placement targets the **door**, not the room: a reader guards a passage, not a
space. That is physically accurate, and it answers `guards` immediately — a
room-targeted drop would not say which boundary is controlled. **At least one
of the door's rooms surfaces**, so the reader never sits on a boundary to
nowhere.

At least one, not both, because of the apartment entrances. Every flat has a
reader on its door from the hallway, and surfacing both sides would surface
every flat's entrance room — seven per floor — which is precisely the clutter
this section exists to avoid. So the hallway side surfaces and the apartment
side does not: a room whose envelope is an apartment stays hidden, and the flat
remains the thing the map shows. Everywhere else both sides surface, which is
why a gym's locker room and the gym floor outside it both appear.

**What stops a room being folded away is being guarded, not holding hardware.**
A device sitting in a room is the flat's — an answering unit draws with the
flat whether or not the room around it is on the map — so it is no reason to
keep that room visible. A device on a room's door is only a reason if it
verifies access *into that room*: a walk-through room carries the readers for
the rooms beyond it, and those rooms are the boundaries being checked. On this
floor plan the hallway carries seven apartment entrance readers and not one of
them guards the hallway, so the hallway folds and the seven flats do not.

While a group is collapsed, interior **doors** reveal on demand — faint glyphs,
no walls, no room fills. Far less clutter than revealing full geometry, and it
is exactly the set of things that can receive a device.

Two manual overrides remain, as exception paths:

- **Show separately** — a room with no hardware that a manager wants labelled
- **Show all rooms** — a *view* toggle, per group, reverts on Esc, stores nothing
- **Hide rooms without devices** — a *reset*, discards manual surfacing, states
  how many, undoable. Rooms with devices never hide: a reader in an invisible
  room is a boundary nobody can see

An apartment and a gym are the **same object** — a group with a different
`space_type`. Only the default room visibility differs. Keeping them as separate
kinds would mean every consumer dispatching on which one it has, forever, for no
gain.

## Adding to groups

Grouping is one field:

```python
room.group_id = target_group
```

This is why merge is non-destructive. Combining two flats does not delete walls
or doors — the rooms are reassigned, inner walls become derived-hidden, and
splitting them back gives exactly the original geometry because nothing was ever
destroyed.

**Merge** (A absorbs B): reassign B's rooms, move B's devices and residents,
mark `B.domain_ref` orphaned rather than hard-deleting it so undo remains
possible.

**Split**: `split_group(g, room_ids)` → a new group with those rooms, plus a
name and type. No door work at all — every door was already an access edge, so
nothing becomes one.

If the split line crosses somewhere with no door, the two groups are not
connected. That is correct, and the user should be told to add one.

Arbitrary-cut division remains available for when segmentation under-splits a
space, but it is the fallback, not the main gesture — and it is the fragile part,
so demoting it is a gain.

**Rooms never leave their envelope on account of a device.** A locker room with a
reader stays under the gym, in the tree and on the map. Splitting it out is an
administrative act — it needs its own `domain_ref`, its own name, its own billing
or tenancy — not something a reader triggers. That separation is the reason
access is checked per room rather than per envelope.

## Envelopes inside envelopes

Envelopes nest to any depth:

```python
@dataclass
class Group:
    parent_group_id: str = None      # None → top level on its floor
```

```
Floor 0
├─ Lobby                       (envelope)
│  └─ Entrance hall            (room)
└─ Gym                         (envelope)
   ├─ Gym corridor             (room)
   ├─ Changing rooms           (envelope)
   │  ├─ Locker room M         (room, reader on its door)
   │  └─ Locker room W         (room, reader on its door)
   └─ Fitness hall             (room)
```

**Nesting is free because envelopes carry no access meaning.** The access graph is
over rooms, and every door is an edge, so adding a level changes nothing in
`build_adjacency` or in the traversal. That is the payoff of moving access to room
level: the organisational hierarchy and the access structure are independent, so
one can be arbitrarily deep without complicating the other.

Only two things walk the chain:

```python
def access_type(room, groups):
    if room.space_type:
        return room.space_type
    g = groups[room.group_id]
    while g.space_type is None and g.parent_group_id:
        g = groups[g.parent_group_id]
    return g.space_type

def rooms_in(group, groups, rooms):            # recursive — includes descendants
    direct = [r for r in rooms if r.group_id == group.id]
    return direct + [r for c in children(group) for r in rooms_in(c, ...)]
```

So a room inherits its type from the nearest ancestor that defines one. A changing
room envelope can set `space_type = "locker_room"` once and both lockers inherit
it, rather than typing each room.

Three rules keep it from getting loose:

- **No cycles.** Reject a reparent that would make an envelope its own ancestor.
- **One floor per subtree.** An envelope and all its descendants are on one floor,
  because geometry does not cross floors.
- **`domain_ref` at whichever level the platform recognises.** A flat has one; a
  changing-room envelope inside a gym probably does not. An envelope with no
  `domain_ref` is purely visual grouping, which is fine.

Reordering and reparenting in the drawer are both safe — nothing derived reads
`parent_group_id` except the two functions above, and neither affects geometry or
reachability.

## Reordering the navigation drawer

`sort_index` on floors and groups, inserted at the midpoint between neighbours
so one record is written rather than a whole list renumbered.

Nothing derived reads `sort_index`, which is the test for whether a reorder can
break anything.

The one trap: **vertical doors pair adjacent `level`s, not adjacent drawer rows.**
Dragging L5 above L3 must not relink them — that fabricates an elevator. Rebuild
vertical doors when a `level` changes, a floor is added or removed, a
`shaft_id` is set, or a space leaves the stairs or lift category; never on
reorder. Show `level` in the drawer row so an order of 3, 5, 4 does not look
like a bug.

**A shaft is edited through the category, not a tool of its own.** A space is
part of the vertical circulation because it is in the stairs or lift category,
and the plan draws it so — treads for a stair, a cross for a lift shaft. A tool
that tagged shafts separately would be a second place for the same fact to live,
free to disagree with the drawer.

Taking a space out of those categories clears its envelope's `shaft_id` and
regenerates the links, which is where pairing adjacent levels earns itself:
dropping a middle storey leaves two shorter shafts, not one shaft with a long
step past a floor that is no longer in it. That is a change to what the access
search can reach, so **confirm it first, and say which storeys stop being
linked** — a warning about "the structure" in the abstract tells a manager
nothing they can check against the building.

The reverse is not symmetrical. Giving some room the stairs category says what
it is, not which stairwell it belongs to, and a building can have several;
joining a shaft needs the shaft named.

Groups cannot move between floors. Geometry would not follow, since floors have
independent coordinate systems.

## What an operation refuses to do

Every refusal below is checked *before* anything is written, so a rejected
operation leaves the model untouched. The message names the actual reason — a
generic failure leaves a manager with no way forward.

**Dividing a room**

- the cut line may not pass through a door
- both cut endpoints must land on walls of the room being divided
- a self-intersecting or doubling-back cut polyline is rejected at input, not
  mid-operation (detected by the walk reaching the other cut wall before the
  second endpoint)

**Adding a door**

- it must fit: `t ± width/2` within `[0, 1]` on its wall
- it may not overlap an existing door on the same wall — two doors stacked would
  render as one wide hole
- it may not sit on a slit wall (`room_a == room_b`), which has no two sides

**Deleting a wall (merging rooms)**

- the wall must have a room on both sides; one facing the exterior would open
  the room to outside rather than merge it with anything
- the merge must not close around a third room: *these two wrap around the
  bathroom, so merging them would shut it inside the new room while it is
  still a space of its own*
- a caller that names the surviving room, or the surviving envelope, must name
  one of the two actually involved
  envelope survives rather than guessing, and re-bind `domain_ref` afterwards.
  An apartment against a plain group needs no question — the apartment wins

**Devices**

- every id in `guards` must be one of the door's two rooms — catches a stale
  value left by an earlier edit
- a device whose `guards` becomes empty is flagged, not deleted: an unguarded
  reader on a real door is a configuration error worth surfacing

**Structure**

- a space type may not be deleted without reassigning its spaces — an untyped
  space has undefined access semantics
- no two rooms on one floor share a `shaft_id`
- a shaft with a single member gets no vertical doors; flag it, since it usually
  means the other floors were not tagged
- reparenting an envelope may not create a cycle

## Undo

The last **five operations** are kept, each with enough state to reverse it:

```python
@dataclass
class Operation:
    kind: str                  # "delete_wall", "divide", "add_door", "regroup"...
    affected: dict             # full records as they were, keyed by id
    created: list              # ids to remove on undo
```

`affected` holds **complete records, not diffs**, because several operations
destroy information that cannot be reconstructed from the resulting state:

- **collinear dissolve** removes a vertex entirely, so a merge's entry must carry
  the dissolved vertex ids and the pre-dissolve wall ids — the resulting single
  wall gives no clue where the join was
- **door `t` rescaling** on split and dissolve is lossy in the same way; store the
  values as they were
- **wall deletion** destroys the wall, its doors, and the second room's record
- **device orphaning** must restore `host_id` and `guards` together

Recording the operation's *inputs* is not enough. "Merge room A and B" does not
describe what the merge destroyed, because the void check, the name choice, and
the dissolve all depend on state that is gone afterwards.

Grouping operations are the cheap case — `room.group_id` is one field, so the
entry is one value.

Five is a deliberate cap, not a technical limit: it covers the realistic
"that was wrong, go back" window without implying a full history the prototype
does not have.

## Assumptions worth stating explicitly

1. **Doors with no device are freely passable.** Defensible, but a reviewer will
   ask.
2. **Access is room-granular.** Movement inside a *room* is free, so a slit wall
   blocks physically but not in the access model. Sub-room pathfinding is a
   different problem. Envelopes carry no access meaning at all — they are
   organisational, which is what lets a room stay inside one regardless of the
   hardware on its doors.
3. **A room's `domain_ref` is display only.** Residents, devices and permissions
   belong to the group.
4. **The platform's structure is the source of truth.** The visual layer points
   into it. Import binds zones to existing entities; it never invents them, or a
   corrected re-upload would duplicate records.
5. **Unbound is a valid state.** A segmented, not-yet-assigned space should
   render distinctly — it is a real thing a manager must resolve. The same holds
   for an orphaned device: unplaced is a state, not an error to clean up.

## Deliberately out of scope

**Wall dragging.** Vertices are shared, so moving one reshapes neighbouring
rooms — a single drag can visibly deform three spaces the user was not touching.
Doing it properly needs per-frame validation (no room collapsing, no
self-intersection, wall length not dropping below its doors' total width),
cascading endpoint slides constrained to adjacent wall directions, and an undo
entry capturing every affected vertex's prior position.

The cheaper substitute, if geometry adjustment turns out to be needed: **numeric
resize** — select a wall, type a distance, the wall translates along its normal.
Identical model changes, no drag loop, trivially reversible. It answers whether
users want to adjust geometry at all before the gesture is built.

Adding and removing walls covers the common case regardless: segmentation misses
a partition far more often than it places one wrongly.

**Sub-room pathfinding.** Movement inside a room is free, so a slit wall blocks
physically but not in the access model.

**Holes as voids.** Every enclosed region is a room, never empty space. If
segmentation produces a polygon with a hole and no room filling it, synthesise an
unassigned room rather than carrying a void through — voids make every geometry
operation conditional.
